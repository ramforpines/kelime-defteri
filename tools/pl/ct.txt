@B1 | A useful habit
Many people say they do not have enough time to read books. One simple solution is to carry a book with you wherever you go. You will be surprised how many minutes you spend waiting every day, for example at the bus stop or in a queue at the bank. If you use these short moments to read, you can easily finish a book every month. It is also a good idea to keep your phone in your bag, because it is much harder to concentrate when messages keep arriving. After a few weeks, reading will become a normal part of your day.

@B2 | Working from home
Over the past few years, millions of office workers have started working from home for at least part of the week. Supporters of this change argue that it saves time and money, since employees no longer have to travel to work every day. Many people also say that they can concentrate better without the constant noise and interruptions of a busy office. However, working from home is not without its problems. Some employees find it difficult to separate their working life from their private life, and they end up answering emails late at night. Others miss the informal conversations with colleagues that often lead to new ideas. As a result, many companies are now looking for a balance between the two.

@B2 | City gardens
In many large cities, people are turning empty spaces into small community gardens. These gardens are often created on the roofs of buildings or on land that has not been used for years. Local residents grow vegetables, fruit and flowers, and they share both the work and the harvest. Supporters say that the gardens improve the quality of the air and provide a home for bees and other insects. Perhaps more importantly, they bring together neighbours who might otherwise never have spoken to each other. Some schools have even started using these gardens to teach children where their food actually comes from.

@C1 | Sleep and memory
Scientists have long suspected that sleep plays a crucial role in the formation of long-term memories. Recent research suggests that during deep sleep the brain replays the experiences of the day, strengthening some connections between neurons and weakening others. This process appears to help us decide which information is worth keeping and which can safely be forgotten. In one study, participants who were allowed to sleep after learning a list of words remembered considerably more of them than those who stayed awake. Interestingly, the benefit was greatest for information that the participants had been told would be useful in the future. Such findings have obvious implications for students who habitually stay up all night before an exam.

@C1 | The attention economy
In an age of constant digital connection, human attention has become one of the most valuable resources in the world. Technology companies compete fiercely for every minute we spend looking at our screens, because our attention can be sold to advertisers. To achieve this, many applications are deliberately designed to be difficult to put down, using techniques borrowed from psychology and even from the gambling industry. Critics argue that this business model has serious consequences for our ability to concentrate, as well as for our mental health. Some designers who helped build these systems have since become their most vocal opponents. They are now calling for regulations that would make technology serve its users rather than exploit them.

@C2 | Maps and power
Maps are often regarded as neutral representations of the world, yet they have always reflected the priorities of the people who drew them. A cartographer must inevitably decide what to include and what to leave out, and these decisions are rarely as innocent as they appear. During the colonial era, for instance, maps frequently depicted vast territories as empty, conveniently ignoring the communities that had inhabited them for centuries. Even the choice of projection carries implications, since flattening a sphere onto a page necessarily distorts the relative size of continents. It is hardly surprising, therefore, that the redrawing of borders has so often been accompanied by bitter disputes. To read a map critically is to ask not only what it shows, but whose interests it serves.

@C2 | The paradox of choice
Conventional economic wisdom holds that the more options consumers have, the better off they will be. A growing body of psychological research, however, suggests that an abundance of choice can be paralysing rather than liberating. When confronted with dozens of nearly identical products, many people find it so difficult to reach a decision that they postpone it indefinitely or abandon it altogether. Those who do eventually choose are frequently less satisfied with their selection, haunted by the suspicion that one of the rejected alternatives might have been superior. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced among so-called maximisers, who feel compelled to examine every possibility before committing themselves. Paradoxically, then, limiting our options may sometimes be the surest route to contentment.
