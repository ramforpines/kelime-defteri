=== B2 | A radio interview: a volunteer

F: What does your work involve?

F: Is it ever difficult?
M: Of course. Sometimes patients I've got to know very well don't get better, and that's hard. But the staff are very supportive, and we have regular meetings where volunteers can talk about how they're feeling.

=== B2 | Lecture introduction: sleep and learning
F: Today I want to talk about something that every student does, but very few think about carefully — sleep. We often treat sleep as wasted time, something we can cut back on when we're busy. In fact, research shows that sleep plays an active role in learning. While we sleep, the brain sorts through the information we've taken in during the day, strengthening the important memories and discarding the rest. That's why staying up all night before an exam is usually a bad strategy: you may gain a few hours of revision, but you lose the process that helps you remember it. Later in the lecture, I'll look at some practical ways of improving sleep, but first, let's look at what actually happens in the brain.
Q: What common attitude to sleep does the speaker criticise?
* That it is time that could be better used.
- That students sleep too much.
- That sleep needs are exactly the same for everyone.
Q: What does the brain do during sleep, according to the speaker?
* It selects which memories to keep.
- It forgets everything learned that day.
- It rests completely without activity.
Q: What will the speaker talk about next?
* What happens in the brain during sleep.
- Some practical tips for improving sleep.
- How to revise for exams.

=== C1 | Podcast: the cost of convenience
M: We've become used to having almost anything delivered to our door within a day, sometimes within an hour. It's incredibly convenient, and I'm not going to pretend I don't use these services. But I think we rarely stop to consider the hidden costs. There's the environmental impact, obviously — all those vans making individual deliveries, and the enormous amount of packaging. But there's also a human cost. Many delivery drivers are classed as self-employed, which means they don't get sick pay or holiday pay, and they're often under intense pressure to meet unrealistic targets. And then there's the effect on local shops, which simply can't compete on speed. None of this means we should stop ordering online altogether. But it might mean asking ourselves whether we really need something tomorrow, or whether it could wait a few days.
Q: What is the speaker's attitude to delivery services?
* He uses them but thinks people ignore their drawbacks.
- He refuses to use them at all for ethical and environmental reasons.
- He believes they have no real disadvantages.
Q: What problem for drivers does he mention?
* They lack basic employment benefits.
- They earn more than shop workers.
- They are not allowed to work long hours.
Q: What does he suggest at the end?
* Thinking about whether fast delivery is really necessary.
- Stopping all online shopping immediately.
- Only ever buying things from small local shops.

=== C1 | Interview: an architect
F: Your buildings are known for using a lot of natural light. Why is that so important to you?
M: Partly it's practical — good natural light reduces the need for electric lighting, which cuts energy use. But mainly it's about how people feel. There's a lot of evidence that daylight affects mood, concentration and even sleep patterns. When you design an office or a school, you're shaping how hundreds of people will spend most of their day, and I think that's a responsibility you have to take seriously.
F: Isn't there a risk of buildings becoming too hot, with all that glass?
M: Absolutely, and that's been a real problem with some modern buildings. The answer isn't to use less glass, necessarily, but to think carefully about orientation and shading — where the sun will be at different times of day and year. It's more work at the design stage, but it saves enormous problems later.
Q: What is the architect's main reason for using natural light?
* Its effect on people's wellbeing.
- It is cheaper to build.
- Clients always ask for it in their contracts.
Q: What responsibility does he mention?
* Buildings influence how people spend their days.
- Architects must keep costs as low as possible.
- Schools must follow strict design rules.
Q: How does he suggest avoiding overheating?
* Careful planning of direction and shade.
- Using much less glass in buildings.
- Installing powerful air conditioning.

=== C2 | Talk: the myth of the lone genius
M: Popular history loves the image of the lone genius — the solitary scientist or inventor who, through sheer brilliance, transforms the world. It's a compelling story, but it is, for the most part, a myth. Almost every major breakthrough turns out, on closer inspection, to have depended on a wider network: collaborators whose names have been forgotten, rivals whose work provided the crucial clue, and institutions that supplied funding and equipment. The myth persists partly because it makes for better storytelling, and partly because prizes and patents tend to reward individuals rather than teams. But it has real consequences. Young researchers who believe that success depends on innate brilliance may be discouraged by early setbacks, and organisations that celebrate individual stars may neglect the conditions that actually make innovation possible.
Q: What is the speaker's main argument?
* Breakthroughs usually depend on many people and institutions.
- Most famous inventors secretly stole their best ideas from other people.
- Individual brilliance is the main source of progress.
Q: Why does the myth persist, according to the speaker?
* It makes a better story and fits how rewards are given.
- Historians have deliberately hidden the truth.
- Teams generally prefer not to share the credit with their leaders.
Q: What harmful effect of the myth does he mention?
* It may discourage young researchers who struggle at first.
- It makes scientists unwilling to spend any time working alone.
- It reduces the amount of funding for research.

=== C2 | Conversation: the ethics of travel
F: I've been feeling increasingly uneasy about flying so much for holidays. The environmental cost is hard to justify.
M: I understand that, but I'm not convinced that giving up travel entirely is the answer. Tourism is the main source of income for a lot of poorer countries. If everyone in wealthy countries stopped visiting, the people who'd suffer most wouldn't be us.
F: That's a fair point, though it can also be used as a convenient excuse. And a lot of the money from tourism goes to international hotel chains rather than local communities.
M: True. So perhaps the question isn't whether to travel but how — fewer, longer trips, staying in locally owned places, travelling by train where it's possible.
F: I suppose that's more realistic than giving it up altogether. Although I suspect most people won't change unless flying becomes more expensive.
Q: What argument does the man make against giving up travel?
* Poorer countries depend heavily on tourists.
- Flying is less harmful than people think.
- Travel is essential for personal development.
Q: How does the woman respond to his argument?
* She accepts it partly but notes it can be misused.
- She rejects it completely as an argument with no value.
- She agrees that it ends the discussion.
Q: What does the woman predict at the end?
* Behaviour will change mainly if prices rise.
- Most people will soon stop flying voluntarily.
- Train travel will become more popular than flying.
