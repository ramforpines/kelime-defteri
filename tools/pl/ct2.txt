# Seviye tespiti — C-test ek metinler

@A2 | My best friend
My best friend is called Deniz and we have known each other since primary school. She lives in the next street, so we often walk home together after school. Deniz is very good at drawing and she wants to be an artist one day. At the weekend we sometimes go to the cinema or meet our friends in the park. She is always happy and she makes me laugh a lot. When I have a problem, she listens to me and gives me good advice. I think everyone needs a friend like her.

@A2 | A busy Saturday
Last Saturday was a very busy day for my family. In the morning we went to the market to buy fruit and vegetables for the week. Then my mother and I cleaned the house while my father washed the car. After lunch my little brother had a football match, so we all went to watch him play. His team won three to one and he scored a goal. In the evening we were very tired, so we ordered pizza and watched a film at home. I went to bed early that night.

@A2 | The new café
A new café opened in our town last month, and it is already very popular. It is in the main square, next to the old library. The café sells coffee, tea, cakes and sandwiches, and the prices are not too high. There are big windows, so it is bright and warm inside. On Friday evenings a local band plays music there. My friends and I go there every week after our English class. We usually sit near the window and talk for hours. I really like the chocolate cake there.

@A2 | Learning to swim
When I was eight years old, I could not swim at all. Every summer my family went to the seaside, but I was afraid of the water. One year my uncle decided to teach me. At first we stayed near the beach where the water was not deep. He held my hands and showed me how to move my legs. After a few days I could swim a short distance alone. I was very proud of myself. Now swimming is one of my favourite sports and I go to the pool twice a week.

@B1 | Volunteering
Last year I decided to do some volunteer work at an animal shelter near my home. I had always loved animals, but I had never looked after them properly before. Every Saturday morning I helped to feed the dogs, clean their cages and take them for walks in the park. At first it was quite hard work, and I was often very tired when I got home. However, I soon realised how much I enjoyed it. Some of the dogs had been badly treated in the past, so it was wonderful to see them become happier and more confident. Two of them have now found new homes with loving families.

@B1 | Moving to a new town
Moving to a new town can be exciting, but it can also be quite stressful. When my family moved last year, I was worried that I would not make any friends. I did not know anyone at my new school and everything seemed strange. During the first week I spent most of my breaks alone. Then one day a girl in my class invited me to join the school choir. I was nervous at first, but I decided to accept. Through the choir I met lots of friendly people, and now I feel completely at home here.

@B1 | Healthy habits
Most people know that they should eat well and exercise regularly, but changing our habits is not always easy. Experts say that it is better to make small changes rather than trying to change everything at once. For example, you could start by walking to work two days a week instead of taking the bus. You could also try to drink more water and eat an extra portion of vegetables every day. These changes might seem small, but over time they can make a big difference to your health. The most important thing is to choose habits that you can keep for a long time.

@B1 | A day without phones
Last month our school organised a day without mobile phones. All the students had to leave their phones at home or give them to the teachers in the morning. Many of us were worried because we use our phones for almost everything. At first it felt strange not to check messages during the breaks. However, after a while we started talking to each other much more than usual. Some students played games in the playground, while others read books in the library. At the end of the day most of us agreed that it had been a really positive experience.

@B2 | The rise of online learning
In recent years, online learning has grown enormously in popularity, offering people the chance to study almost anything from the comfort of their own homes. Courses are available on subjects ranging from computer programming to creative writing, and many of them are free of charge. One of the main advantages is flexibility, since learners can choose when and where they study. This makes it particularly attractive to people who have jobs or family responsibilities. On the other hand, studying alone requires a great deal of self-discipline, and many people find it hard to stay motivated without the support of a teacher or classmates. For this reason, a large number of online students never actually complete their courses.

@B2 | Food waste
Every year, around a third of all the food produced in the world is either lost or wasted. In wealthy countries, a large proportion of this waste happens in people's homes, where food is often thrown away simply because it has passed its best-before date. Many people do not realise that this date refers to quality rather than safety, so the food may still be perfectly fine to eat. Supermarkets also contribute to the problem by rejecting fruit and vegetables that do not look perfect. Fortunately, attitudes are beginning to change, and a growing number of organisations now collect unwanted food and give it to people in need.

@B2 | The importance of sleep
Although most adults need between seven and nine hours of sleep a night, many regularly get far less than that. Long working hours, stress and the use of electronic devices late in the evening are all common reasons for this. Lack of sleep does not only make us feel tired; it can also affect our mood, our memory and our ability to make good decisions. Over a long period, it has been linked to serious health problems such as heart disease and obesity. Doctors recommend going to bed at the same time each night and avoiding screens for at least an hour beforehand.

@B2 | Fast fashion
Over the past twenty years, the way we buy clothes has changed dramatically. Thanks to so-called fast fashion, shoppers can now buy the latest styles at very low prices, and many people buy new clothes every month. However, this convenience comes at a cost. The production of cheap clothing uses enormous amounts of water and energy, and much of it ends up in landfill after being worn only a few times. In addition, workers in some factories are paid very low wages and work in poor conditions. As consumers become more aware of these issues, second-hand shopping and clothes-swapping events are becoming increasingly popular.

@C1 | Urban wildlife
Cities are often thought of as hostile environments for wildlife, yet a surprising number of species have adapted remarkably well to urban life. Foxes, for example, have become a familiar sight in many European cities, where they feed on food waste and shelter in gardens and parks. Some birds have even altered their songs, singing at a higher pitch so that they can be heard above the constant noise of traffic. Researchers believe that cities may actually offer certain advantages, such as warmer temperatures and fewer natural predators. Nevertheless, urban development continues to threaten many habitats, and conservationists argue that green spaces should be carefully protected and connected.

@C1 | The psychology of habits
According to psychologists, a significant proportion of our daily behaviour is driven by habit rather than conscious decision. Once a habit has been established, it is triggered automatically by a particular cue, such as a time of day or a specific location, which explains why habits can be so difficult to break. Interestingly, research suggests that the most effective strategy is not to rely on willpower alone, but to change the environment in which the behaviour occurs. Someone trying to eat more healthily, for instance, might simply avoid keeping sweets in the house. Over time, the new behaviour becomes the default, requiring far less mental effort to maintain.

@C1 | Remote communities
For centuries, isolated communities in mountainous regions developed distinctive languages, customs and forms of music, largely untouched by the outside world. In recent decades, however, improved roads and the spread of the internet have brought these communities into closer contact with modern society. While this has undoubtedly provided access to better healthcare, education and economic opportunities, it has also accelerated the decline of traditional ways of life. Many young people now leave for the cities in search of work, and some local languages are spoken only by the elderly. Efforts to document and revive these cultures are under way, but their long-term success remains uncertain.

@C1 | The value of boredom
In a world of constant stimulation, boredom has come to be regarded as something to be avoided at all costs. Whenever we have a spare moment, we instinctively reach for our phones in search of distraction. Yet a growing body of research suggests that boredom may serve a valuable purpose. When the mind is not occupied with a specific task, it tends to wander, and this process appears to be closely linked to creativity and problem-solving. In one experiment, participants who had first completed a deliberately dull task went on to generate more original ideas than those who had not. Perhaps, then, we should learn to tolerate boredom rather than escape it.

@C2 | Translation and meaning
Translation is frequently portrayed as a straightforward process of replacing words in one language with their equivalents in another, but anyone who has attempted it seriously knows otherwise. Languages carve up reality in subtly different ways, and a term that seems perfectly ordinary in one culture may carry connotations that have no counterpart elsewhere. Humour, wordplay and poetry pose particularly formidable challenges, since their effect depends as much on sound and rhythm as on literal meaning. The translator must therefore make countless judgements, weighing fidelity to the original against readability in the target language. It is perhaps unsurprising that many regard translation as an art rather than a science.

@C2 | Memory and the self
We tend to think of memory as a faithful record of the past, yet decades of research have demonstrated that it is remarkably malleable. Each time we recall an event, we reconstruct it, and in doing so we may unwittingly alter details, incorporate information acquired later, or even invent episodes that never occurred. This has profound implications, not least for the reliability of eyewitness testimony in criminal trials. It also raises intriguing philosophical questions, since our sense of identity rests largely on the stories we tell ourselves about our own lives. If those stories are partly fictional, the self may be less stable than we imagine.

@C2 | The ethics of automation
As machines become capable of performing tasks once thought to require human judgement, societies are being forced to confront difficult ethical questions. Who, for instance, should be held accountable when an autonomous vehicle causes an accident: the manufacturer, the programmer or the owner? Equally contentious is the question of employment, since automation threatens to render entire professions obsolete while generating enormous wealth for a relatively small number of companies. Some economists advocate a universal basic income as a means of sharing these gains more equitably, whereas others maintain that new forms of work will inevitably emerge, as they have after previous technological revolutions.

@C2 | The appeal of nostalgia
Nostalgia was once classified as a medical condition, a form of melancholy suffered by soldiers and travellers far from home. Today, by contrast, psychologists increasingly view it as a largely beneficial emotion that can strengthen social bonds and lend a sense of continuity to our lives. Recalling cherished moments from the past appears to counteract loneliness and to bolster self-esteem, particularly during periods of uncertainty. Advertisers have not been slow to exploit this tendency, reviving familiar brands and designs in the hope of evoking warm associations. Critics caution, however, that an excessive attachment to an idealised past may hinder our willingness to embrace change.
