=== A2 | Lost dog
P: LOST DOG! Our dog Max went missing on Tuesday evening near Green Park. He is a small brown dog with white feet and a red collar. He is three years old and very friendly, but he is afraid of loud noises, so please do not shout or run after him.
P: If you see Max, please call Anna on 07700 900 812 at any time, day or night. Please don't try to catch him — just tell us where he is. There is a reward of £100 for anyone who helps us find him. Thank you!
Q: What should you do if you see Max?
* Phone Anna and say where he is.
- Catch him and take him home.
- Call his name loudly.
Q: Why shouldn't people run after Max?
* Noise frightens him.
- He might bite them.
- He runs very fast.
Q: When can people call Anna?
* Whenever they want.
- Only in the evening.
- Only on Tuesdays.

=== B1 | Starting a podcast
P: When my friend Jake suggested we start a podcast about football, I thought it was a terrible idea. There are already thousands of football podcasts, and neither of us knew anything about recording or editing. But Jake was so enthusiastic that I eventually agreed to try one episode.
P: The first recording was a disaster. We forgot to turn on one of the microphones, so half the conversation was lost, and when we listened back, we both talked far too fast. It took us three attempts to produce something we were happy with. To our surprise, about fifty people listened to it in the first week — mostly friends and family, I suspect.
P: Two years later, we have more than five thousand regular listeners. I think the secret is that we focus on our local team, which the big podcasts never mention. Listeners also like that we're honest: we're fans, not experts, and we're not afraid to admit when we're wrong.
Q: How did the writer feel when Jake first suggested the podcast?
* He didn't think it was a good idea.
- He was excited and wanted to start immediately.
- He was worried about how much it would cost.
Q: What went wrong with the first recording?
* Part of it wasn't recorded.
- Nobody listened to it at all.
- They talked about the wrong team.
Q: Who probably listened to the first episode?
* People the writers already knew.
- Football experts from other podcasts.
- Fans of the local team.
Q: Why does the writer think the podcast became popular?
* It covers a team other podcasts ignore, and it's honest.
- The writers are well-known football experts.
- It is shorter than most other football podcasts.

=== B1 | The repair café
P: Every Saturday morning, the community hall in our village turns into a "repair café". Local volunteers with different skills — electricians, tailors, carpenters — sit at tables and help people fix broken things for free. Visitors bring everything from toasters and lamps to torn jackets and wobbly chairs.
P: The idea is not only to save money but also to reduce waste. "So many things are thrown away because of a small problem that takes ten minutes to fix," says organiser Ruth Evans. "And people learn something too. We don't just repair things for them — we show them how to do it themselves."
P: Not everything can be saved, of course. Some modern electronic products are designed in a way that makes them very difficult to open, and spare parts can be impossible to find. But Ruth says that around three out of four items leave the café working again.
Q: What happens at the repair café?
* Volunteers help people fix their things without charging.
- People buy second-hand toasters and lamps.
- Volunteers sell repaired items to raise money.
Q: What does Ruth say about the volunteers' approach?
* They teach visitors to do repairs themselves.
- They prefer to repair things without visitors watching.
- They only repair things that are very old.
Q: Why can some modern products not be repaired?
* They are hard to open and parts are not available.
- The volunteers are not allowed to touch electronics.
- Their owners don't want to wait long enough.
Q: How successful is the café, according to Ruth?
* Most items are fixed.
- About a quarter of items are fixed.
- Almost nothing modern can be fixed.

=== B1 | Learning a language with films
P: Many language learners watch films and series in the language they are studying, but is it actually useful? According to teacher Sara Lopez, it can be — as long as you do it in the right way. "Just watching for fun with subtitles in your own language doesn't help much," she says. "Your brain simply reads the subtitles and ignores the sound."
P: Instead, she recommends watching with subtitles in the language you're learning. This helps you connect the written and spoken forms of words. She also suggests choosing a series rather than films, because you get used to the characters' voices and the vocabulary is often repeated from episode to episode.
P: Sara's final tip is to watch short scenes more than once. "The first time, just try to follow the story. The second time, pay attention to particular phrases and try repeating them out loud."
Q: Why does Sara think subtitles in your own language are not very helpful?
* Learners stop paying attention to what they hear.
- They are often badly translated.
- They make films more difficult to follow.
Q: Why does she recommend series rather than films?
* Learners become familiar with the voices and words.
- Series are usually shorter and easier.
- Films use too many difficult accents.
Q: What should learners do the second time they watch a scene?
* Focus on specific expressions and say them aloud.
- Turn off the subtitles completely.
- Write down every word they don't know.

=== B2 | Why we love lists
P: Open any news website and you are likely to find articles with titles such as "Ten Habits of Successful People" or "Seven Places You Must Visit Before You're Thirty". These so-called listicles have become one of the most popular forms of online content, and psychologists have several explanations for their appeal.
P: The first is that lists promise a clear, limited amount of information. Before we even start reading, we know exactly how long the article will take and what we will get from it, which makes the decision to click feel low-risk. The second is that lists are easy to scan; readers can jump to the items that interest them and skip the rest. Numbers in headlines also seem to suggest that the information has been carefully selected and organised, even when it has not.
P: Critics argue that the format encourages shallow thinking. Complex subjects are reduced to a handful of simple points, and the connections between ideas are often lost. Nevertheless, editors continue to use lists for one simple reason: they attract readers.
Q: According to the text, why does clicking on a list feel "low-risk"?
* Readers know in advance what they will get and how long it will take.
- Lists are always written by experts.
- Lists are shorter than other news articles.
Q: What impression can numbers in headlines create?
* That the content has been carefully chosen.
- That the article is very recent.
- That the writer is very successful.
Q: What do critics say about the format?
* It oversimplifies complicated topics.
- It makes articles too long to read.
- It is less popular than it used to be.
Q: Why do editors keep using lists, according to the writer?
* They are effective at attracting an audience.
- They are cheaper to produce than other articles.
- Readers have asked for more of them.

=== B2 | The rise of board games
P: In an age of smartphones and video games, it might seem surprising that traditional board games are enjoying a boom. Sales have grown steadily for more than a decade, and cafés where customers can pay to play from a library of hundreds of games have opened in cities around the world.
P: Part of the explanation lies in the games themselves. Modern board games are very different from the ones many people remember from childhood, in which success depended largely on the roll of a dice. Today's games often involve complex strategy, cooperation between players, or elaborate storylines, and they attract adults as much as children.
P: However, many players say the real attraction is social. "When you play a video game online, you're often alone in a room," explains one café owner. "With a board game, you're sitting round a table with your friends, laughing and arguing. It's a reason to put the phones away."
Q: What evidence of the boom is mentioned?
* Rising sales and new cafés dedicated to games.
- More children giving up video games.
- Board games replacing smartphones in schools.
Q: How are modern board games different from older ones?
* They depend more on skill and planning than on luck.
- They are designed mainly for young children.
- They are played mostly online.
Q: According to the café owner, what is the main appeal of board games?
* They bring people together face to face.
- They are cheaper than video games.
- They are easier to learn than video games.

=== C1 | The placebo effect
P: The placebo effect — the improvement in symptoms that some patients experience after receiving a treatment with no active ingredient — has long been regarded as a nuisance in medical research. New drugs must outperform a placebo in clinical trials, and the effect is often treated as little more than a statistical complication. Increasingly, however, researchers are taking it seriously as a phenomenon in its own right.
P: What makes the effect so intriguing is how sensitive it is to context. Placebo injections tend to produce stronger effects than placebo pills, and expensive-looking pills appear to work better than cheap ones. Even the manner of the doctor matters: patients treated by warm, attentive practitioners often report greater improvement than those treated by brisk, distant ones.
P: Perhaps most surprisingly, some studies suggest that placebos can work even when patients know they are taking them. In trials of so-called "open-label" placebos for conditions such as chronic back pain, patients who were told honestly that their pills contained no medicine still reported meaningful relief. Researchers are cautious about drawing firm conclusions, but such findings raise intriguing questions about the role of expectation and ritual in healing.
Q: How has the placebo effect traditionally been viewed in medical research?
* As an inconvenience to be controlled for.
- As the most important part of any treatment.
- As a dangerous side effect of new drugs.
Q: What do the examples in the second paragraph show?
* The effect depends on how a treatment is presented.
- Cheap pills are more effective than expensive ones.
- Patients prefer pills to injections.
Q: What is surprising about "open-label" placebos?
* They can help even when patients know there's no medicine.
- They only work for patients who are lied to.
- They have stronger effects than real drugs.
Q: What is the researchers' attitude to these findings?
* Interested but careful not to overstate them.
- Completely convinced that placebos should replace drugs.
- Dismissive, because the studies were too small.

=== C1 | Handwriting in the digital age
P: As keyboards and touchscreens have become the default tools for writing, many schools have reduced the time devoted to teaching handwriting, and some have dropped joined-up writing altogether. To many, this seems a sensible adjustment to modern life. Yet a number of researchers argue that something important may be lost in the process.
P: Several studies have found that students who take lecture notes by hand remember the material better than those who type. The explanation appears to be that handwriting is slower, forcing students to summarise and rephrase information rather than recording it word for word. This additional processing, in turn, helps the information to be understood and retained.
P: There is also evidence that learning to form letters by hand helps young children learn to read, as the physical movement reinforces their recognition of letter shapes. Critics of these studies point out that the differences are often modest and that typing has obvious advantages in speed and legibility. Most researchers, however, are not calling for a return to the past, but for a balance in which handwriting retains a place alongside digital skills.
Q: Why, according to the research, do handwritten notes aid memory?
* Writing slowly makes students process the information.
- Handwritten notes are easier to read later.
- Students write more words by hand than they type.
Q: What benefit of handwriting for young children is mentioned?
* It helps them recognise letters when reading.
- It improves their typing speed later.
- It makes them more interested in books.
Q: What do most researchers recommend?
* Teaching both handwriting and digital skills.
- Replacing keyboards with handwriting in schools.
- Stopping the teaching of joined-up writing.

=== C2 | The attention we pay to risk
P: Human beings are notoriously poor judges of risk. We fear flying more than driving, despite the overwhelming statistical evidence that road travel is far more dangerous, and we worry more about rare, dramatic threats such as terrorist attacks than about mundane ones such as heart disease. Psychologists attribute much of this to the "availability heuristic": we judge the likelihood of an event by how easily examples come to mind, and vivid, widely reported events are disproportionately memorable.
P: Other factors compound the distortion. Risks that are imposed on us provoke more anxiety than those we choose to take, which is why people who would never accept a nuclear power station near their home may happily ski or ride motorcycles. Likewise, risks that seem to be under our control feel smaller than they are; most drivers consider themselves more skilful than average, a statistical impossibility.
P: These biases are not merely academic curiosities. They shape public policy, since governments under pressure to respond to well-publicised dangers may divert resources from less visible but more lethal ones. A more rational allocation would require not only better data but also a willingness to confront the uncomfortable gap between what frightens us and what actually harms us.
Q: What does the "availability heuristic" explain?
* Why memorable events seem more probable than they are.
- Why people prefer flying to driving.
- Why rare events are reported less often.
Q: Why might someone reject a nearby power station but enjoy skiing?
* People tolerate risks they choose more readily than imposed ones.
- Skiing is statistically safer than nuclear power.
- Power stations are more dramatic than ski accidents.
Q: What does the writer say about drivers' self-assessment?
* Most of them overestimate their own ability.
- Most of them are better than average.
- Most of them underestimate road dangers deliberately.
Q: What consequence of these biases is described in the last paragraph?
* Resources may be spent on visible rather than deadly risks.
- Governments ignore public opinion about danger.
- Better data has already solved the problem.

=== C2 | The afterlife of words
P: Languages are in constant flux, and few things provoke such indignation among a certain kind of commentator as a word that appears to have changed its meaning. The use of "literally" as an intensifier, as in "I literally died of embarrassment", is a perennial target, condemned as a corruption that renders the word useless. Yet lexicographers point out that this usage is far from new; it can be found in the works of celebrated nineteenth-century novelists.
P: Semantic change, they argue, is not a symptom of decline but the ordinary mechanism by which languages adapt. "Nice" once meant "foolish"; "awful" meant "inspiring wonder". Such shifts are frequently driven by the very processes that purists deplore: exaggeration, irony and the gradual weakening of emphatic words through overuse.
P: This is not to say that every innovation deserves acceptance, nor that concerns about clarity are illegitimate. Ambiguity can genuinely impede communication, particularly in legal or technical contexts. But the history of English suggests that attempts to freeze the language in place have rarely succeeded, and that today's solecism is quite often tomorrow's standard usage.
Q: What point do lexicographers make about "literally" used for emphasis?
* It has a long history in respected writing.
- It was invented by modern commentators.
- It has made the word impossible to use.
Q: According to the second paragraph, what often causes semantic change?
* The same habits that purists object to.
- Deliberate decisions by dictionary makers.
- The influence of legal and technical language.
Q: What is the writer's view of concerns about clarity?
* They are sometimes justified, especially in specialised contexts.
- They are always an excuse for resisting change.
- They should lead to laws protecting the language.
