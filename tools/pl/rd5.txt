=== A2 | Email: a weekend in the city

In the afternoon the sun came out and we walked up a big hill. The view of the city was beautiful! Tomorrow we're going home by plane because it's quicker. I'll show you my photos next week. Love, Ella

- By plane.
- By car.
Q: Why did they go to the museum?

- It was close to the hotel.

=== B1 | A letter to the editor
P: Dear Editor, I am writing about the council's plan to close the swimming pool in Bridge Street. The council says the pool is too expensive to repair and that people can use the new sports centre instead. However, the sports centre is on the edge of town, and there is no direct bus from the town centre.
P: Many people who use the Bridge Street pool are elderly or have young children, and they cannot easily travel that far. The pool is also used by three local schools for swimming lessons. If it closes, these children will have to travel by coach, which will cost the schools money and take time away from other lessons.
P: I understand that the council has to save money, but I believe there are other options, such as raising the entrance fee slightly or asking local businesses for support. I hope the council will think again before making a final decision. Yours faithfully, Peter Hall
Q: Why does the council want to close the pool?
* Repairs would cost too much.
- Not enough people use it.
- A new pool is being built in Bridge Street.
Q: What problem does Peter mention about the sports centre?
* It is difficult to get to without a car.
- It is more expensive than the old pool.
- It doesn't have a swimming pool.
Q: How would the closure affect local schools?
* They would have to pay for transport.
- They would have to stop swimming lessons.
- They would need to build their own pools.
Q: What does Peter suggest?
* Looking for other ways to pay for the pool.
- Closing the sports centre instead.
- Offering free entry to all the schools in the area.

=== B1 | Learning to drive
P: I failed my driving test three times before I finally passed. The first time, I was so nervous that I forgot to look in the mirror before changing lanes. The second time, I drove too slowly on the motorway, and the examiner said I was causing problems for other drivers. The third time, I made a small mistake when I was parking and then panicked.
P: Before my fourth test, my instructor suggested something different. Instead of only practising driving, I spent time learning to stay calm, using breathing exercises before each lesson. She also took me on the actual test routes, so that I knew exactly what to expect.
P: On the day of the test, I felt much more relaxed. I still made one small mistake, but this time I didn't let it affect the rest of my drive — and I passed.
Q: What happened during the writer's second test?
* She didn't drive fast enough.
- She forgot to use the mirror.
- She had a problem with parking.
Q: What was different about the preparation for the fourth test?
* She worked on controlling her nerves.
- She had more lessons on the motorway.
- She changed her driving instructor.
Q: Why did she pass the fourth test?
* She stayed calm after making an error.
- She didn't make any mistakes at all.
- The examiner was more relaxed.

=== B2 | The rise of the side hustle
P: An increasing number of people with full-time jobs are also running small businesses on the side, from selling handmade jewellery online to offering weekend photography services. Surveys suggest that in some countries as many as one in three young adults has such a "side hustle".
P: For some, the motivation is mainly financial. Rising rents and living costs mean that a single salary no longer stretches as far as it once did. For others, a side project offers something their main job does not: the chance to be creative, to be their own boss, or to test a business idea without giving up a steady income.
P: Yet the trend has a darker side. Critics argue that the celebration of side hustles on social media turns every hobby into a potential source of income, leaving people feeling guilty for simply relaxing. There are also concerns about burnout, as people who work evenings and weekends on top of a full-time job may have little time left for rest, friends or family.
Q: What is a "side hustle", according to the text?
* A business people run in addition to their main job.
- A second job that people do only at weekends for an employer.
- A full-time job working from home.
Q: Apart from money, why do some people start side hustles?
* To have freedom and creativity their main job lacks.
- To meet new friends and people outside their usual work.
- To escape from high rents.
Q: What concern do critics raise about social media?
* It makes people feel that free time should earn money.
- It encourages people to leave their jobs.
- It makes most side businesses much less successful than before.
Q: What risk to health is mentioned?
* Exhaustion from working too much.
- Injuries from physical work.
- Stress caused by losing a job.

=== B2 | The joy of walking
P: Walking is perhaps the most underrated form of exercise. It requires no special equipment, costs nothing, and can be fitted into almost any daily routine. Yet because it feels so ordinary, many people do not think of it as exercise at all.
P: The health benefits, however, are considerable. Regular brisk walking has been shown to reduce the risk of heart disease, improve sleep and lower stress levels. Some studies suggest that a daily walk of just thirty minutes can have a measurable effect on mood, particularly when it takes place in green spaces such as parks or woodland.
P: Walking also appears to stimulate thinking. Many famous writers and philosophers were enthusiastic walkers, and recent experiments have found that people come up with more creative ideas while walking than while sitting. For this reason, some companies now encourage "walking meetings", in which colleagues discuss ideas while strolling outdoors rather than sitting around a table.
Q: Why, according to the writer, do people underestimate walking?
* It seems too normal to count as exercise.
- It takes too much time each day.
- It is less healthy than other sports.
Q: When does walking seem to have the greatest effect on mood?
* When it takes place in natural surroundings.
- When it lasts for more than an hour.
- When people walk together with friends or family.
Q: Why have some companies introduced "walking meetings"?
* Walking may help people think more creatively.
- Offices have become too small for meetings.
- Employees need more physical exercise.

=== C1 | The paradox of choice in dating apps
P: Dating apps promised to revolutionise romance by giving users access to an almost unlimited pool of potential partners. In many respects they have succeeded: a substantial proportion of couples now meet online. Yet many users report feeling more dissatisfied and exhausted than ever, and some researchers believe that the sheer abundance of choice may be partly to blame.
P: When options seem endless, people may become reluctant to commit, always wondering whether someone better is just one swipe away. The format of the apps can also encourage rapid, superficial judgements, reducing complex individuals to a handful of photographs and a short description. Some users describe the experience as resembling online shopping more than meeting people.
P: In response, a number of newer apps deliberately limit the number of matches users receive each day, or require them to answer detailed questions before seeing photos. Whether such changes can overcome the underlying tension between convenience and meaningful connection remains to be seen.
Q: What problem do some researchers associate with dating apps?
* Too many options can make people less satisfied.
- Too few people use them to find partners.
- They are far too expensive for most of their users.
Q: Why might users avoid committing to one person?
* They suspect a better match may be available.
- They prefer to meet people in real life.
- They are worried about their safety when meeting online.
Q: How are some newer apps responding?
* By restricting choice or discouraging quick judgements.
- By showing users more photographs.
- By charging users a small fee for each new match they receive.
Q: What is the writer's view of these changes?
* It is not yet clear whether they will work.
- They have already solved the problem.
- They are certain to make apps less popular.

=== C1 | The decline of letter writing
P: Not so long ago, the handwritten letter was a central means of personal communication. Today, outside of formal occasions, it has been almost entirely replaced by email and instant messaging. Few would wish to return to the days of waiting weeks for a reply, yet something may have been lost in the transition.
P: Historians, in particular, have reason to be concerned. Much of our knowledge of past lives comes from letters, which often reveal thoughts and feelings that were never recorded elsewhere. Digital messages, by contrast, are scattered across platforms, frequently deleted, and stored in formats that may become unreadable within a few decades. Future historians may therefore find it harder to reconstruct our private lives than we find it to reconstruct those of our great-grandparents.
P: There is also a difference in the nature of the communication itself. Because letters took effort and time, they encouraged reflection; writers tended to consider their words carefully. Instant messages, by their nature, favour speed and brevity, and are often composed in fragments.
Q: What does the writer suggest about the move from letters to digital messages?
* It has had some negative consequences.
- It has made communication slower.
- Most people want to return to writing letters.
Q: Why might future historians face difficulties?
* Digital messages may not survive.
- People today write fewer messages.
- Letters are often lost in the post.
Q: How were letters different from instant messages, according to the text?
* They encouraged people to think before writing.
- They were usually shorter and more frequent.
- They were mainly used for formal occasions.

=== C2 | The cult of productivity
P: Few virtues are extolled as relentlessly in contemporary culture as productivity. Bookshops overflow with guides promising to help readers accomplish more in less time, and an entire industry of apps exists to track, optimise and gamify the working day. Beneath this apparently benign enthusiasm, however, lies an assumption worth questioning: that the value of a life can be measured by its output.
P: Critics trace the problem partly to the blurring of boundaries between work and leisure. When smartphones make it possible to answer emails at any hour, rest itself can come to feel like a dereliction of duty, and even hobbies may be pursued with an eye to self-improvement rather than enjoyment. The result, paradoxically, is often diminished effectiveness, as chronic overwork erodes the concentration and creativity on which genuinely productive work depends.
P: None of this amounts to an argument for idleness. Rather, it suggests that the pursuit of efficiency, when elevated to an end in itself, risks crowding out the very experiences — unstructured time, deep conversation, aimless curiosity — that give work its purpose in the first place.
Q: What assumption does the writer question?
* That a person's worth depends on how much they produce.
- That productivity apps are harmful to everyone.
- That people today work much less than people did in the past.
Q: According to critics, what effect does technology have?
* It makes resting feel like failing in one's duties.
- It makes hobbies more enjoyable.
- It gradually improves people's concentration over time.
Q: What paradox is described in the second paragraph?
* Constant work can make people less effective.
- Leisure time makes people more stressed.
- Productive people always end up with much more free time.
Q: What does the writer conclude?
* Efficiency should not become a goal in its own right.
- People should stop working and do nothing at all.
- Unstructured time is a waste of resources.

=== C2 | Nature writing and its discontents
P: Nature writing has enjoyed a remarkable resurgence in recent years, with books about birdwatching, wild swimming and solitary walks regularly appearing on bestseller lists. Admirers credit the genre with reawakening a sense of wonder at the natural world and with fostering environmental awareness at a time of ecological crisis.
P: Not everyone is persuaded. Some critics contend that much contemporary nature writing presents the countryside as a refuge for the individual soul, a place of personal healing, while largely ignoring the people who live and work there. Farmers, foresters and rural communities rarely appear, except as obstacles to the writer's solitude. Others note that the genre has historically been dominated by a narrow range of voices, and that the experience of nature it describes — leisurely, contemplative, safe — is not equally available to everyone.
P: Defenders respond that the best nature writing has always been more complex than its caricature, attentive to history, labour and loss as well as beauty. The challenge for the genre, perhaps, is to reconcile its lyrical impulse with a more searching account of the landscapes it celebrates.
Q: Why do admirers value contemporary nature writing?
* It renews people's appreciation of nature.
- It gives accurate scientific information.
- It encourages people to move to the countryside.
Q: What criticism is made in the second paragraph?
* The genre often overlooks those who depend on the land.
- The genre focuses too much on farming.
- The genre greatly exaggerates the many dangers of the natural world.
Q: What does the writer suggest the genre needs to do?
* Combine its poetic qualities with deeper understanding.
- Abandon its focus on beauty altogether.
- Concentrate only on the personal experiences of the writer.

=== C2 | The return of the long read
P: When digital media first began to dominate the news industry, conventional wisdom held that online readers would tolerate nothing longer than a few hundred words. Attention spans, it was claimed, had been fatally shortened by the constant distractions of the internet, and publishers responded accordingly, breaking stories into ever smaller fragments.
P: Yet the past decade has witnessed an unexpected counter-trend. Long-form journalism — in-depth investigations and essays running to many thousands of words — has found a substantial and loyal audience, and several publications have built successful subscription models around it. Analytics suggest that readers who engage with such pieces tend to spend far longer on a site and are more likely to pay for content.
P: The explanation may lie partly in saturation. As short, instantly forgettable content has proliferated, its value has diminished, while the scarcity of careful, considered reporting has made it more prized. It would be premature, however, to declare the death of the soundbite; the two forms appear destined to coexist, serving different needs.
Q: What did publishers originally believe about online readers?
* They would only read very short pieces.
- They preferred long investigative articles.
- They would refuse to pay for any content.
Q: What do analytics show about readers of long-form pieces?
* They are more engaged and more willing to pay.
- They visit sites less frequently.
- They prefer free content to subscriptions.
Q: How does the writer explain the trend?
* Thoughtful content has become valuable because it is rare.
- Readers' attention spans have increased dramatically.
- Short content is now completely banned on many news sites.
Q: What does the writer predict?
* Short and long content will both continue.
- Short content will soon disappear completely.
- Long-form journalism will replace all news.
