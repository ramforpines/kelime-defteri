=== C1 | Lecture: the history of the umbrella
F: The umbrella is such an ordinary object that it's easy to forget how controversial it once was. In eighteenth-century London, men who carried umbrellas were often mocked in the street. The umbrella was associated with women and with the French, and using one suggested that you couldn't afford a carriage. Interestingly, the hostility also came from coach drivers, who saw umbrellas as a threat to their business — after all, one of the reasons people hired coaches was to stay dry. It was only towards the end of the century, thanks largely to a few persistent individuals who carried them despite the ridicule, that umbrellas became respectable. By the nineteenth century, a black umbrella had become almost a symbol of the English gentleman.

- Umbrellas were considered dangerous in crowded streets.
- Umbrellas were too expensive for ordinary people.
Q: Why were coach drivers against umbrellas?
* Umbrellas might reduce the demand for coaches.
- Umbrellas frightened their horses in the busy streets.
- Umbrellas made the streets too crowded.
Q: How did umbrellas eventually become accepted?
* Some people kept using them despite being laughed at.
- The government encouraged people to use them.
- Coach drivers began to sell them to their passengers.

=== C1 | Talk: why we procrastinate online
M: Most of us have had the experience of opening a laptop to do some work and finding, forty minutes later, that we've done nothing except read the news and check social media. It's tempting to see this as a lack of willpower, but the design of the tools we use plays a significant part. Many apps are deliberately built to be hard to leave, using techniques such as infinite scrolling and unpredictable rewards, the same principle that makes slot machines so addictive. What this means in practice is that relying purely on self-control is often a losing battle. A more effective approach is to change the environment — for example, using a separate device or account for work, or blocking certain sites during working hours. In other words, rather than trying to resist temptation, it's usually easier to remove it.
Q: According to the speaker, why do people waste time online?

Q: What comparison does the speaker make?

- Social media is very similar to reading a printed newspaper.
- Laptops are like televisions.
Q: What does the speaker recommend?
* Making temptations harder to access.
- Practising self-control every day.
- Working for shorter periods.

=== C2 | Lecture: the economics of happiness
M: For much of the twentieth century, economists assumed that rising incomes would translate fairly directly into rising wellbeing. The economist Richard Easterlin challenged this in the 1970s when he observed that, although richer people within a country tended to be happier than poorer ones, average happiness in many countries had barely changed despite decades of economic growth. Several explanations have been proposed. One is that people judge their circumstances relative to those around them, so that if everyone becomes richer, no one feels much better off. Another is adaptation: we quickly grow accustomed to improvements, and what once felt like a luxury soon comes to seem normal. More recent research has complicated the picture, suggesting that growth does increase wellbeing in poorer countries, but the paradox remains a powerful challenge to the assumption that more is always better.
Q: What did Easterlin observe?
* National growth did not seem to raise average happiness much.
- Poorer people were generally happier than the richer people in their country.
- Happiness rose steadily with economic growth.
Q: What does the "relative" explanation suggest?
* People compare themselves with others around them.
- People always prefer luxuries to the basic things they need.
- People feel happier in poorer countries.
Q: How has recent research changed the picture?
* Growth appears to matter more in poorer countries.
- It has completely disproved Easterlin's ideas.
- It shows that adaptation does not exist.

=== C2 | Interview: a translator of poetry
F: Is it even possible to translate poetry?
M: I think it's possible to produce something valuable, but not to produce the same poem. Every translation involves losses. Sometimes you can preserve the rhythm but not the exact meaning, sometimes the reverse. The translator's job is to decide which losses matter least for a particular poem.
F: How do you decide?
M: I ask what the poem is really doing. If it depends on a play on words, I'll try to find an equivalent joke in English, even if it means changing the words entirely. A literal translation would be more accurate in a narrow sense, but it would kill the poem.
F: Some critics say that's taking too much freedom.
M: They do, and they have a point — there's a risk of the translator's voice replacing the poet's. But I'd rather produce something that lives in English than something that's technically faithful but lifeless.
Q: What does the translator believe about translating poetry?
* Something is always lost, so choices must be made.
- A good translation can be identical to the original.
- Only the rhythm of a poem can be translated.
Q: What does he do with a play on words?
* Create a similar joke in English.
- Translate it word for word as accurately as possible.
- Leave it out completely.
Q: How does he respond to his critics?
* He accepts their concern but defends his approach.
- He says that his critics simply don't understand poetry at all.
- He has decided to change his method.

=== C2 | Talk: museums and returned objects
F: Few issues in the museum world are as contentious as the question of whether objects acquired during the colonial era should be returned to their countries of origin. For a long time, major European museums resisted such demands, arguing that their collections were universal, accessible to visitors from around the world, and that they possessed the expertise to preserve fragile objects. These arguments have lost much of their force. Many countries of origin now have well-equipped museums of their own, and the claim to universality sits uneasily with the fact that most of the world's population will never be able to visit London or Paris. Increasingly, the debate has shifted from whether objects should be returned to how — through permanent restitution, long-term loans or shared ownership arrangements.
Q: What argument did European museums traditionally use?
* Their collections served people from all over the world.
- The objects had all been bought legally and at fair prices at the time.
- Countries of origin did not want the objects back.
Q: Why does the speaker say the "universal" argument is weak?
* Most people in the world cannot visit those museums.
- European museums now have very few visitors from other countries.
- The objects are rarely put on display.
Q: How has the debate changed?
* The focus is now on the method of return.
- Most countries have stopped asking for objects.
- Museums now refuse all requests.

=== B2 | Podcast: learning to cook
F: I didn't learn to cook until I was nearly thirty, and I was embarrassed about it for years. Then I realised that most of the cookbooks I'd tried assumed you already knew the basics. They'd say things like "cook the onions until soft" without explaining how long that takes or what "soft" actually looks like. So my first piece of advice is to find recipes aimed at real beginners, ideally with videos. Second, don't try to cook a different dish every night. Master five or six simple recipes you actually enjoy, and repeat them until you don't need the instructions. That's how you build confidence. And finally, don't worry about mistakes. Some of my worst disasters taught me more than my successes.
