=== B2 | The comeback of vinyl
P: Twenty years ago, most people assumed that vinyl records were finished. CDs had taken over, and then digital downloads and streaming services seemed to make physical music formats completely unnecessary. Yet in recent years, vinyl sales have risen every year, and in some countries they now outsell CDs.
P: What explains this unexpected revival? For many buyers, it's about the experience rather than the sound. Putting on a record, looking at the large cover artwork and listening to an album from beginning to end feels like an event, in contrast to the endless, often distracted scrolling of streaming. Some fans also claim that vinyl sounds "warmer", although sound engineers point out that this is largely a matter of taste.
P: Interestingly, the typical vinyl buyer is not an older person feeling nostalgic for their youth. Surveys suggest that a large proportion are under thirty, and that many of them also use streaming services every day. For these listeners, records are not a replacement for digital music but a way of owning and displaying the music that matters most to them.
P: Not everyone is convinced the trend will last. Records are expensive to produce, factories struggle to meet demand, and prices have risen sharply. Critics also note that many buyers rarely play the records they buy.
Q: What does the writer say about the popularity of vinyl twenty years ago?
* It was widely expected to disappear.
- It was more popular than CDs.
- It was mainly bought by young people.
Q: According to the text, what attracts many people to vinyl?
* The ritual of listening to music in a focused way.
- The fact that it is cheaper than paying for streaming services.
- Scientific proof that it sounds better.
Q: What do surveys show about vinyl buyers?
* Many are young and stream music as well.
- Most are older people remembering the past.
- Most have stopped using streaming services.
Q: What does "this" refer to in the second paragraph ("this is largely a matter of taste")?
* The idea that vinyl has a warmer sound.
- The experience of looking at cover artwork.
- The habit of scrolling through streaming apps.
Q: Which concern about the trend is mentioned in the last paragraph?
* Production problems are pushing prices up.
- Young people are losing interest quickly.
- Streaming services are becoming cheaper.

=== B2 | Learning from failure
P: In many schools, mistakes are treated as something to be avoided. Students who get a wrong answer may feel embarrassed, and marks are often taken away for every error. But a growing number of educators argue that this approach discourages exactly the kind of thinking that leads to real learning.
P: Research in psychology suggests that people often remember information better when they have first made a mistake and then been corrected, especially if they were confident about their original answer. The surprise of being wrong seems to make the correct information stick. Some teachers have therefore started giving students difficult problems before explaining how to solve them, accepting that most students will initially fail.
P: Critics worry that this method could damage students' confidence, particularly those who already struggle. Supporters respond that the key is how failure is presented. If students understand that mistakes are a normal part of the learning process, rather than a sign that they are not clever enough, they are more likely to keep trying.
P: The challenge for schools, of course, is that exams still reward getting the right answer first time. Until assessment systems change, teachers who encourage risk-taking may find themselves under pressure to return to more traditional methods.
Q: What is the main criticism of the traditional approach to mistakes?
* It prevents students from thinking in ways that help them learn.
- It gives students far too many difficult problems to solve alone.
- It makes exams too easy for most students.
Q: According to research, when are corrections most effective?
* When a person felt sure their wrong answer was right.
- When the teacher explains the answer before the problem.
- When students make as few mistakes as possible.
Q: How do supporters respond to the concerns of critics?
* The way failure is explained to students is what matters.
- Students who struggle should be given easier tasks.
- Confidence is less important than getting high marks.
Q: What problem is mentioned in the last paragraph?
* Exams don't reward the kind of learning this method encourages.
- Teachers don't have enough time to give students useful feedback.
- Students prefer traditional methods of teaching.

=== B2 | The problem with ratings
P: Before booking a hotel, choosing a restaurant or buying a pair of headphones, most of us now check online ratings. In theory, these reviews give us honest information from people who have already tried the product. In practice, however, they can be surprisingly misleading.
P: One problem is that people who write reviews are not typical customers. Those who have had either a wonderful or a terrible experience are far more likely to write something than those who were simply satisfied. As a result, ratings often cluster at the extremes, and a product with an average of four stars may have very few four-star reviews at all.
P: Another issue is fake reviews. Some businesses pay people to post positive comments about their products, or negative ones about their competitors. Although websites use software to detect suspicious reviews, experts estimate that a significant proportion still get through.
P: So what should consumers do? Experts suggest ignoring the overall score and instead reading several of the more moderate reviews, which tend to contain more detailed and balanced information. It is also worth checking when the reviews were written, since a restaurant may have changed owners or chefs since its best reviews appeared.
Q: Why are online ratings often unrepresentative?
* Customers with extreme experiences are more likely to write reviews.
- Most customers give four stars without thinking carefully about it.
- Websites delete the most critical reviews.
Q: What does the writer say about websites' detection software?
* It doesn't catch all false reviews.
- It removes almost every fake review.
- It is mainly used against competitors.
Q: What do experts recommend?
* Paying attention to the less extreme reviews.
- Choosing products with the highest scores.
- Only reading the most recent five-star reviews.
Q: Why is it useful to check the dates of reviews?
* The business may be different now.
- Older reviews are more likely to be fake.
- Recent reviews are always more detailed.

=== B2 | Night owls and early birds
P: Some people leap out of bed full of energy at six in the morning, while others can barely function before ten but feel at their best late at night. For a long time, these differences were seen as a matter of habit or even discipline. Scientists now know, however, that they are partly determined by our genes.
P: Each of us has an internal body clock, known as a circadian rhythm, which controls when we feel sleepy and alert. In so-called "night owls", this clock runs a few hours later than in "early birds". Our natural rhythm also changes with age: teenagers tend to become later types, which may explain why many of them struggle with early school start times.
P: This has led some schools to experiment with later starts. In one American study, moving the start of the school day from 7.30 to 8.30 led to improved attendance and slightly better grades. Students also reported feeling less sleepy in class.
P: However, changing the timetable is not always simple. Parents' working hours, school bus schedules and after-school activities all depend on the existing routine, and some families have opposed the changes for practical reasons.
Q: What has science shown about early birds and night owls?
* Their differences are partly inherited.
- They can easily change their habits.
- Night owls are less disciplined.
Q: What does the text say about teenagers?
* Their body clocks tend to shift later.
- They are naturally early birds.
- They need less sleep than adults.
Q: What was one result of the American study?
* Students attended school more regularly.
- Students' grades improved dramatically.
- Students went to bed much earlier.
Q: Why have some families been against later school starts?
* It would cause problems with their daily routines.
- They believe teenagers need more discipline, not more sleep.
- They think grades would get worse.

=== B2 | Tiny houses
P: In several countries, a growing number of people are choosing to live in so-called "tiny houses" — homes that are often smaller than thirty square metres. Some are built on wheels so that they can be moved from place to place, while others sit on small plots of land at the edge of towns.
P: For many owners, the main attraction is financial. A tiny house can cost a fraction of the price of a conventional home, allowing people to avoid large mortgages and, in some cases, to stop renting altogether. Others are drawn by environmental concerns: smaller homes use far less energy for heating and lighting, and owners tend to buy fewer possessions simply because there is no room for them.
P: Living in such a small space is not for everyone, though. Several owners admit that the first few months were difficult, particularly for couples, who had to learn to share a very limited area. Finding somewhere to put a tiny house can also be a challenge, as planning laws in many areas were not designed with such homes in mind.
Q: Why do many people choose tiny houses?
* They are much cheaper than normal homes.
- They are easier to sell than flats.
- They are always built in city centres.
Q: According to the text, why do tiny-house owners buy less?
* They simply have nowhere to keep things.
- They have less money after paying the mortgage.
- They are not allowed to own many things.
Q: What difficulty is mentioned for couples?
* Getting used to sharing very little space.
- Agreeing on where to move the house each time they travel.
- Paying for two separate houses.
Q: What problem is caused by planning laws?
* They make it hard to find a legal place for the house.
- They require every tiny house to be built on wheels so it can move.
- They limit tiny houses to thirty square metres.

=== B2 | The museum that lets you touch
P: Most museums have a strict rule: look, but don't touch. A small museum in the north of England has decided to do the opposite. Visitors are encouraged to pick up, hold and examine most of the objects on display, from ancient tools to Victorian toys.
P: The idea came from the museum's director, who noticed that visitors, particularly children, quickly lost interest when objects were kept behind glass. "People learn through their hands as well as their eyes," she says. "When you hold a two-thousand-year-old coin, you feel a connection to the person who used it that you simply can't get from reading a label."
P: Of course, the approach has risks. A few items have been damaged, and the most valuable or fragile objects are still kept in cases. But the museum argues that most of its collection consists of everyday items of which many similar examples exist, so the benefit to visitors outweighs the occasional loss. Since the change, visitor numbers have doubled.
Q: Why did the director introduce the new policy?
* Visitors seemed to become bored with objects behind glass.
- The museum had far too many objects to display behind glass.
- Children kept breaking the glass cases.
Q: What does the director believe about touching objects?
* It creates a stronger feeling of connection to the past.
- It is more useful for adults than reading the labels carefully.
- It helps protect objects from damage.
Q: Why does the museum accept the risk of damage?
* Most objects are common and could be replaced.
- All valuable objects have already been sold.
- Visitors pay extra if they break something.
Q: What has happened since the change?
* Twice as many people now visit.
- Fewer objects are damaged.
- The museum has moved to a bigger building.
