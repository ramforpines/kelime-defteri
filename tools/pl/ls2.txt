=== B2 | Choosing a university course
F: So have you decided which course you're going to apply for?
M: Not really. My parents want me to study law, because they think it'll lead to a well-paid job. But to be honest, I'm much more interested in environmental science.
F: Couldn't you do both? Some universities offer joint degrees.
M: I looked into that, but it would take five years instead of three, and I can't really afford the extra time.
F: Well, have you spoken to anyone who works in environmental science? It might help to find out what the job's actually like.
M: That's a good point. My cousin works for a conservation charity. I've been meaning to ask her for ages.
F: Do it this week. The application deadline's at the end of the month.

* They think it offers better financial prospects.
- They both work as lawyers and want him to join them.
- They think he would enjoy it more.
Q: Why has the man rejected a joint degree?
* It would take too long.
- No university offers one.
- It is too expensive.
Q: What does the woman suggest?
* Talking to someone with experience in the field.
- Applying to both courses separately at different universities.
- Waiting another year before deciding.
Q: What has the man been intending to do?
* Contact his cousin.
- Visit a university.
- Speak to his parents again.

=== B2 | A podcast about running
M: When I started running five years ago, I made every mistake possible. I bought cheap shoes, I ran too far too soon, and within a month I'd injured my knee. So my first piece of advice is: don't try to do too much at the beginning. Your heart and lungs adapt quite quickly, but your muscles and joints take much longer, and that's when injuries happen. The second thing is to run slowly — slower than you think. Most beginners run at a pace that's far too hard, and they end up hating it. You should be able to hold a conversation while you're running. And finally, find a running partner or a club. On cold, dark mornings, knowing that someone's waiting for you is the best motivation there is.
Q: What happened when the speaker first started running?
* He hurt himself after a short time.
- He couldn't run very far at all.
- He found it easy and enjoyed it.
Q: Why, according to the speaker, do beginners get injured?
* Their joints and muscles need more time to adapt than their lungs.
- They don't warm up properly and they never stretch before running.
- They run on hard surfaces in cheap shoes.
Q: How can runners tell if they are running at the right speed?
* They can still talk comfortably.
- They feel slightly out of breath.
- They can run for an hour without stopping.
Q: Why does the speaker recommend running with others?
* It makes you more likely to go out when you don't feel like it.
- It helps you to run faster and further than you could alone.
- It is safer to run in groups at night.

=== B2 | Interview: a young chef
F: You opened your first restaurant at just twenty-four. Wasn't that a huge risk?
M: It was, definitely. Most people told me to wait and get more experience first. But I'd been working in kitchens since I was sixteen, so I felt ready. The real problem wasn't the cooking — it was everything else. I had no idea how to manage staff, deal with suppliers or keep the accounts.
F: So how did you learn?
M: Mostly by making mistakes! In the first year we nearly closed twice. What saved us was a regular customer who happened to be a retired accountant. He offered to look at our finances, and he showed me where we were wasting money.
F: And now you've got three restaurants.
M: That's right. But I still spend at least two evenings a week in the kitchen. I never want to become a chef who just signs the menus.
Q: What does the chef say was most difficult at the beginning?
* Running the business side of the restaurant.
- Cooking for very large numbers of people every evening.
- Finding experienced kitchen staff.
Q: How did a customer help him?
* By giving him advice about money.
- By lending him money to stay open.
- By recommending the restaurant to friends.
Q: Why does he still work in the kitchen?
* He wants to stay involved in the cooking.
- He can't find enough good chefs.
- His customers expect to see him there.

=== C1 | Lecture: the history of tea
M: When we think of tea in Britain, we tend to imagine it as something timeless, almost part of the national character. In fact, it only became widespread in the eighteenth century, and for much of that period it was a luxury. Taxes on tea were so high that smuggling became a major industry; by some estimates, more tea entered the country illegally than legally. It was only when the government drastically cut the tax in 1784 that tea became affordable for ordinary people. What's interesting is how quickly attitudes changed. Within a few decades, tea had gone from being criticised as a wasteful indulgence that would ruin the health of the working classes to being promoted as a respectable alternative to alcohol. And, of course, the demand for tea had enormous consequences far beyond Britain, shaping trade, agriculture and politics in China and India.
Q: What point does the speaker make about tea in Britain?
* Its long history is more recent than people imagine.
- It has always been a drink for ordinary people.
- It was introduced by the government in 1784.
Q: According to the speaker, what was the effect of high taxes?
* A large illegal trade developed.
- People switched to drinking alcohol.
- Tea became more popular among the rich.
Q: How did opinions about tea change?
* It came to be seen as a healthy substitute for alcohol.
- It began to be regarded as a dangerous and unhealthy luxury for the poor.
- It was banned for the working classes.
Q: What does the speaker suggest at the end?
* British tea drinking affected other countries greatly.
- Tea is now more popular in India than in Britain.
- China stopped exporting tea to Britain.

=== C1 | Discussion: remote work
F: I keep reading that remote work is the future, but I'm not sure it suits everyone.
M: No, I agree. For experienced people who already know their jobs, it can be fantastic. But I worry about people at the start of their careers.
F: In what way?
M: Well, so much of what you learn in your first jobs isn't formally taught. You pick it up by overhearing how a colleague handles a difficult client, or by asking a quick question across the desk. That's much harder on a video call — you have to schedule it, and people often don't want to bother anyone.
F: That's true. Although some companies are trying to address it with mentoring schemes.
M: They are, and that helps. But a scheduled meeting once a week isn't really the same as being surrounded by people all day.
F: So you'd say a mixed approach is best?
M: For most organisations, probably. Though it depends enormously on the kind of work involved.
Q: What is the man's main concern about remote work?
* Young employees may miss out on informal learning.
- Experienced staff may become less productive.
- Video calls are too expensive for companies.
Q: Why does he say asking questions is harder online?
* People are reluctant to arrange a call for a small question.
- Video calls often have technical problems that interrupt the conversation.
- Managers don't allow calls during working hours.
Q: What does the man think of mentoring schemes?
* They are useful but not a complete solution.
- They are a waste of time for companies.
- They work better than being in an office.
Q: What is the man's final opinion?
* The best approach varies with the type of job.
- Every company should return to the office.
- Remote work is right for most people.

=== C1 | Radio: a new kind of library
F: In the small town of Oakfield, the local library has started lending something rather unusual: tools. Instead of books, residents can now borrow drills, ladders, sewing machines and even lawnmowers for up to a week, completely free. The idea came from a group of volunteers who realised that most households own equipment they use only a handful of times a year. A typical electric drill, one of them pointed out, is used for less than fifteen minutes in its entire lifetime. The scheme has proved far more popular than expected, with a waiting list for some items. Its organisers argue that it not only saves people money but also reduces waste, and, perhaps unexpectedly, it has brought neighbours together. Several residents now offer short workshops showing others how to use the equipment safely.
Q: What inspired the tool library?
* People rarely use the equipment they own.
- The town's bookshop had closed down.
- Tools in the shops had become too expensive.
Q: What is said about the scheme's popularity?
* Demand for some items exceeds supply.
- Few people have borrowed anything so far.
- It is popular mainly with older residents.
Q: What unexpected benefit is mentioned?
* It has strengthened the local community.
- It has created new jobs in the town.
- It has encouraged people to buy more tools.

=== C2 | Talk: the value of disagreement
F: We often assume that the most effective teams are those in which everyone gets along. Yet a considerable body of research suggests that a certain kind of conflict is not merely tolerable but essential. The crucial distinction is between disagreement about ideas and disagreement about people. Teams that argue vigorously about the substance of a problem, while maintaining mutual respect, consistently make better decisions than those in which members suppress their doubts for the sake of harmony. The danger of excessive consensus — what psychologists call groupthink — is that flawed assumptions go unchallenged until it is too late. The difficulty, of course, is that the two kinds of conflict are easily confused. A criticism of a proposal can be heard as a criticism of the person who made it, and leaders therefore have a particular responsibility to make it clear that dissent is valued rather than punished.
Q: What does the research suggest about successful teams?
* They debate ideas openly while respecting one another.
- They avoid conflict in order to stay harmonious.
- They allow leaders to make all the final decisions.
Q: What is the risk of "groupthink"?
* Mistaken ideas are not questioned in time.
- Personal arguments damage relationships.
- Decisions take too long to make.
Q: Why does the speaker say leaders have a special responsibility?
* People may take criticism of their ideas personally.
- Leaders are usually the source of disagreement.
- Only leaders can stop a discussion.

=== C2 | Interview: a historian on archives
M: You've spent much of your career in archives. What draws you to them?
F: I think it's the sense that history is far less settled than textbooks suggest. When you read the original letters and records, you constantly find details that complicate the familiar story — someone who was supposedly a minor figure turns out to have been pulling the strings, or an event everyone describes as a turning point barely registered at the time.
M: Doesn't that make it hard to reach firm conclusions?
F: It does, and I'd argue that's healthy. The temptation is always to impose a neat narrative on material that resists one. Of course, archives have their own biases. They preserve what institutions and powerful people chose to keep, and the voices of ordinary people, particularly women and the poor, are often missing entirely. So a large part of the work is reading between the lines — noticing who isn't there.
Q: What does the historian enjoy about archives?
* They reveal that the past is more complex than often presented.
- They generally confirm the accounts that are found in most history textbooks.
- They contain mainly the words of ordinary people.
Q: How does she view the difficulty of reaching firm conclusions?
* As something positive.
- As a reason to avoid archives.
- As a problem caused by poor records.
Q: What limitation of archives does she mention?
* Some groups of people are under-represented in them.
- They are too difficult for most historians to access.
- They contain too many personal letters.
