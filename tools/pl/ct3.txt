@A2 | My favourite room
My favourite room in our house is the kitchen. It is not very big, but it is warm and bright because it has a large window. Every evening my family sits at the table and we talk about our day while we eat dinner. My father is a very good cook and he often makes soup with fresh vegetables from the market. On cold winter days I like to do my homework there because it is the warmest room in the house. I think the kitchen is the heart of our home.

@A2 | A rainy day
Last Sunday it rained all day, so we could not go to the park as we had planned. At first my little sister was very sad because she wanted to play outside with her friends. Then my mother had a good idea and we made a big cake together in the kitchen. After that we played board games and watched an old film on the sofa. In the evening the rain stopped, and we went for a short walk to the end of the street. It was a really nice day in the end.

@A2 | My town
I live in a small town in the north of the country, near some beautiful mountains. There are not many shops, but we have a nice market every Wednesday where people sell fruit, cheese and bread. In the summer a lot of tourists come here to walk in the hills and swim in the lake. In the winter it is very cold and sometimes there is snow for many weeks. I like living here because it is quiet and the people are friendly. One day, though, I want to live in a big city.

@A2 | Going to the dentist
Yesterday I went to the dentist because one of my teeth was hurting. I was quite nervous because I don't like dentists very much. The dentist was a kind woman and she explained everything to me before she started. She said that I had a small hole in my tooth and that she could fix it quickly. It did not hurt very much and it only took about twenty minutes. After that she told me to eat fewer sweets and to brush my teeth more carefully every day.

@B1 | Saving money
When I started university, I found it very difficult to manage my money. In the first month I spent almost all of it on eating out and buying new clothes, and by the end I could hardly afford to pay for my bus ticket. So I decided to make a plan and write down everything I spent every week. I was surprised to see how much money I was wasting on small things like coffee and snacks. Now I cook at home more often and I take a flask of coffee with me. As a result, I manage to save a little every month.

@B1 | A festival in my city
Every September my city holds a food festival that lasts for three days. Hundreds of stalls are set up in the main square, and they sell dishes from many different countries around the world. There is also live music in the evenings and special activities for children, such as cooking classes and face painting. Last year more than fifty thousand people visited the festival, so it was quite difficult to move around at times. Even so, I really enjoyed it, and I tried some food that I had never eaten before. I am already looking forward to next year.

@B1 | Working in a team
Working in a team can be very rewarding, but it is not always easy. When people have different opinions, it can take a long time to make decisions, and sometimes arguments start. In my experience, the most important thing is to listen carefully to everyone before you share your own ideas. It also helps if each person has a clear role, so that everyone knows what they are responsible for. When a team works well together, the results are usually much better than when people work alone. That is why many employers look for good team players.

@B1 | Keeping a diary
My grandmother has kept a diary every day for more than fifty years. When I asked her why, she told me that writing helps her to remember the small details of her life that she would otherwise forget. Last year she let me read some of her old diaries, and I learned many things about my family that nobody had ever told me. Inspired by her, I decided to start my own diary in January. At first I often forgot to write, but now it has become a habit. I usually write a few lines before I go to sleep.

@B2 | The value of hobbies
In a busy world where work and responsibilities take up most of our time, hobbies are often the first thing we give up. However, psychologists believe that having a regular hobby can significantly improve our mental health and overall wellbeing. Activities such as painting, gardening or playing a musical instrument give us a sense of achievement that is often missing from our working lives. They also provide a valuable opportunity to switch off from daily worries and focus completely on something we enjoy. What matters most is not how skilled we become, but how much pleasure the activity brings.

@B2 | Public transport
Many cities around the world are trying to persuade people to leave their cars at home and use public transport instead. The benefits are obvious: fewer cars on the roads means less traffic, cleaner air and lower carbon emissions. However, people will only change their habits if public transport is reliable, affordable and convenient. In many places, buses are overcrowded, trains are frequently delayed and tickets are surprisingly expensive. Some cities have responded by making public transport completely free, and early results suggest that this has encouraged many more people to use it, particularly young people and families.

@B2 | Learning from older generations
Young people today have access to more information than any previous generation, yet they may still have a great deal to learn from their grandparents. Older people often possess practical skills that are gradually disappearing, such as repairing clothes, growing vegetables or cooking without a recipe. Perhaps more importantly, they can offer a perspective on life that comes only from experience. They have lived through difficult times and can often explain that problems which seem enormous today will eventually pass. Some schools have started programmes that bring students and older people together to share stories and skills.

@B2 | Working abroad
Working abroad can be one of the most valuable experiences of a person's career, but it also brings significant challenges. Living in a new country means adapting to a different culture, learning new customs and often communicating in a foreign language every day. At first, even simple tasks such as opening a bank account or visiting a doctor can feel surprisingly complicated. Many people also experience loneliness, especially during the first few months when they have not yet made friends. However, those who persevere usually say that the experience made them more confident, flexible and open-minded than before.

@C1 | Citizen science
Over the past two decades, ordinary members of the public have become increasingly involved in genuine scientific research. Through so-called citizen science projects, volunteers count birds in their gardens, classify distant galaxies from telescope images, or record the timing of flowers blooming in spring. Such contributions might seem modest individually, but collectively they generate enormous quantities of data that professional scientists could never gather alone. Participants, for their part, often report a deeper understanding of science and a stronger connection to the natural world. Critics occasionally question the reliability of amateur observations, yet careful project design has largely addressed these concerns.

@C1 | The language of advertising
Advertising language is carefully designed to persuade us, often in ways that we barely notice. Rather than making direct claims that could be challenged, advertisers frequently rely on vague expressions such as "helps to reduce" or "up to fifty per cent", which sound impressive but promise remarkably little. Emotional associations are equally important: products are linked with happiness, success or belonging, encouraging us to buy not the item itself but the lifestyle it seems to represent. Understanding these techniques does not make us immune to them, but it can help us to make more considered decisions as consumers.

@C1 | Volunteering abroad
Every year, thousands of young people travel to developing countries to volunteer on projects such as building schools or teaching English. The motivation is usually admirable, yet the practice has attracted growing criticism in recent years. Some experts argue that short-term volunteers, who often lack relevant skills, may take jobs that could be done by local workers and offer little lasting benefit. In the worst cases, organisations charge high fees while contributing relatively little to the communities involved. Responsible programmes, by contrast, focus on long-term partnerships, respond to local needs and ensure that volunteers' skills genuinely match the work required.

@C1 | Sleep and technology
It has become almost a habit for many people to check their phones last thing at night and first thing in the morning. Researchers have expressed growing concern about the effect of this behaviour on the quality of our sleep. The blue light emitted by screens appears to suppress the production of melatonin, a hormone that helps regulate our sleep cycle, making it harder to fall asleep. Moreover, the content we consume, whether stressful news or exciting messages, can stimulate the mind precisely when it should be winding down. Experts therefore recommend keeping phones out of the bedroom altogether.

@C2 | The ethics of memory
Societies emerging from periods of conflict face an agonising dilemma about how the past should be remembered. Some argue that a full and public reckoning with atrocities is indispensable, both as a matter of justice for victims and as a safeguard against repetition. Others contend that dwelling on historical grievances can perpetuate division, and that a degree of collective forgetting may be the price of lasting reconciliation. In practice, most societies oscillate between these positions, establishing truth commissions and memorials while also granting amnesties. There is no universal formula, and the balance struck tends to reflect the particular circumstances of each conflict.

@C2 | Expertise and its critics
Public trust in expertise has become a peculiarly divisive issue in many democracies. Specialists in fields ranging from medicine to economics find their conclusions dismissed as the opinions of an out-of-touch elite, while unqualified commentators command enormous online audiences. Part of the explanation may lie in high-profile failures of expert prediction, which have understandably damaged confidence. Yet the alternative to imperfect expertise is rarely better judgement; more often it is no judgement at all. Restoring trust will probably require experts to communicate their uncertainty more candidly, acknowledging the limits of their knowledge rather than projecting an authority that invites resentment when it falters.

@C2 | Urban solitude
It is one of the more striking paradoxes of modern life that loneliness should be so prevalent in densely populated cities. Millions of people live within a few metres of one another, yet many report having nobody to confide in. Sociologists attribute this partly to the erosion of institutions that once structured community life, such as religious congregations, trade unions and local clubs. The anonymity that cities afford, once celebrated as liberating, can also become isolating. Some urban planners are now experimenting with shared spaces designed to foster casual encounters, though whether architecture alone can rebuild social bonds remains an open question.

@C2 | The economics of attention
In an economy saturated with information, attention has arguably become the scarcest and most valuable resource. Every notification, advertisement and headline competes for a share of a finite capacity that cannot be expanded at will. Companies have responded by developing increasingly sophisticated techniques for capturing and retaining our focus, often drawing on insights from behavioural psychology. The consequences extend beyond individual distraction: when public debate is shaped by what attracts attention rather than by what deserves it, sensational and polarising content may systematically crowd out nuance. Some theorists therefore argue that attention should be treated as a matter of public concern rather than private choice.
